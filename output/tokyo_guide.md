# A Local Route Through Tokyo (5 Days)

Tokyo rewards a short trip when the days are grouped by neighborhood instead of crossed by taxi. This guide is a 5-day route for a first visit: historic Asakusa, the shrine and shops of Harajuku, a reserved visit to Shibuya Sky, a morning at the Tsukiji outer market, and a night of small plates in Shinjuku. The train advice and the meal table below are meant to be used as you go, not memorized in advance.

## 5 Days in Tokyo

Stay in one hotel for all four nights. Shinjuku, Shibuya, and Tokyo Station are the easiest bases because the Yamanote loop and several subway lines meet there. A hotel beside a single minor station looks cheaper until every dinner requires two transfers.

### Day 1: Asakusa, the Sumida River, and Shibuya after dark

Start at Senso-ji before 9:00. The main hall, the five-story pagoda, and Nakamise-dori are the point of the visit; the covered shopping street is calmer in the first hour, when the snack stalls are opening and tour groups have not filled the lane. Walk toward the Sumida River and cross at Azuma Bridge for the classic view back to the Kaminarimon gate. If the weather is clear, continue to the river promenade. Tokyo Skytree is worth seeing from the ground on this day. Save a paid observation deck for later only if the sky is still open and you have energy left.

Use the afternoon to check into the hotel and reset. In the evening, ride to Shibuya and watch the scramble crossing from the street first, then from a second-floor window of a cafe if you want the elevated view without a timed ticket. Eat near the station rather than commuting again. Shibuya is dense after 19:00, so pick a restaurant with a posted menu and a queue that is still moving.

### Day 2: Meiji Jingu, Harajuku, and Shibuya Sky

Meiji Jingu is the right morning. Enter from the Harajuku side, walk the gravel approach under the trees, and give the inner courtyard a few quiet minutes. The shrine is free. Weekday mornings are the most comfortable; weekends bring wedding processions, which are worth watching from a respectful distance if you happen upon one.

Leave through the opposite side toward Harajuku. Takeshita-dori is a short, crowded lane of crepes and youth fashion. If that street feels too packed, walk Omotesando instead. The boulevard is the same neighborhood with more room and better coffee. Book Shibuya Sky for late afternoon, when the light softens and the observation deck is less harsh than at noon. The ticket is timed. Buy it before you land, and arrive 15 minutes early because the entry line is separate from the purchase.

Dinner can stay in Shibuya or move one stop to Ebisu, which is an easier place to sit down. Do not plan a second major sight after the deck. The view is the event.

### Day 3: Tsukiji, Ginza, and a night in Shinjuku

The inner tuna auction is not the visitor experience it once was. The Tsukiji outer market still is. Go early for tamagoyaki, grilled seafood, and a small sushi counter, then leave before the lanes become a slow shuffle. Walk or take one subway stop to Ginza. The point of Ginza on a short trip is the grid itself: department-store food halls in the basement, side streets with knife shops and stationery, and a long lunch if breakfast was only snacks.

In the afternoon, the East Gardens of the Imperial Palace are free, flat, and a useful pause between two busy districts. Skip the full palace grounds tour unless you already hold a reservation. Late afternoon is for returning to the hotel. Shinjuku at night is the closer: the south side for reliable restaurants, Omoide Yokocho for yakitori alleys, and the west exit only if you enjoy neon more than dinner. Choose one pocket of the station and stay there. Shinjuku is easy to overwalk.

### Day 4: A slower neighborhood, then the airport

Keep the last morning unfinished on purpose. Yanaka Ginza is a sloping shopping street with independent shops and a temple graveyard behind it. Shimokitazawa is the alternative if you would rather browse record shops and small cafes. Either visit fills two hours without a ticket. Be on an airport train with a real buffer. For Narita, leave central Tokyo at least three hours before an international departure, earlier if you are checking bags at the airline counter instead of using a downtown check-in. For Haneda, two and a half hours is a practical target outside the morning rush.

### Day 5: Departure

A 3-night trip fails most often on the ride back to the airport, not at the sights.

## Getting Around Tokyo

Buy a Suica or PASMO card, or add one of those cards to your phone wallet, before the first ride. Tap in and tap out on JR, the two subway companies, and most private lines you will actually use. A paper ticket for every hop wastes the time you saved by booking an observation deck.

The Yamanote line is the green loop around central Tokyo. It is the simplest way to move among Tokyo, Shimbashi, Shibuya, Shinjuku, and Ueno. It is not always the fastest. When a map shows a subway that crosses underneath the loop, that subway is often one train instead of a long arc. Google Maps and Apple Maps both handle this well if you set the departure time. Trust the transfer count more than the station photos.

Rush hour is roughly 7:30–9:30 and 17:30–19:30 on weekdays. Do not haul a suitcase onto the Yamanote then. If your hotel is far from the terminal, send large bags ahead with a luggage-forwarding counter at the airport or the hotel. A same-day or next-morning delivery to Narita or Haneda costs more than a subway ticket and removes the worst part of the last morning.

From Narita, the Keisei Skyliner is the quickest ride into Ueno, and the Narita Express is the simpler ride if your hotel is on the Yamanote loop. From Haneda, the Keikyu line into Shinagawa, or the Tokyo Monorail when it matches your hotel side of the city, is enough. A reserved airport bus is the better choice only when the hotel offers a stop at the door and you are traveling with bags you cannot lift onto a train.

### Local tip: eat inside the station

Major stations hide a whole meal under the ticket gates and in the adjacent department stores. Look for ekiben, the boxed meals sold for train journeys, and for the basement food floors called depachika. At lunch, a station counter is often faster and more consistent than a famous restaurant with a 40-minute wait. Convenience stores are a legitimate breakfast: onigiri, a boiled egg, and canned coffee will carry you to the first sight, which matters more on Day 1 than a hotel buffet.

## Where to Eat in Tokyo

The table is a short list you can actually reach on the route above. Prices are per person for a normal order, before a drink. Ratings here are a traveler's snapshot of consistency, not an official score.

| Category | Recommended Location | Estimated Cost | Rating |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beef cutlet | Gyukatsu Motomura, Shibuya | ¥2,000–¥3,000 | 4.5/5 |
| Ramen | Ichiran, Shibuya or Shinjuku | ¥1,100–¥1,600 | 4.2/5 |
| Yakitori | Omoide Yokocho, Shinjuku | ¥2,000–¥4,000 | 4.3/5 |
| Market breakfast | Tsukiji outer market | ¥1,500–¥3,500 | 4.6/5 |
| Station meal | Tokyo Station ekiben or depachika | ¥800–¥1,800 | 4.4/5 |

Gyukatsu Motomura serves a beef cutlet you finish on a small grill at the table. Go at opening or expect a line that bends down the street. Ichiran is a single-diner booth: you will not have a conversation, and that is the product. Order the classic tonkotsu and leave. It is a reliable bowl, not the best ramen in the city.

Omoide Yokocho is a lantern alley west of Shinjuku Station. The lanes are narrow, some counters are cash-friendlier than others, and the pleasure is skewers plus a beer in a room that seats eight. If a particular counter is full, the next one is ten steps away. At Tsukiji, eat what is cooked in front of you and skip any stall that will not show a price. A standing sushi counter with a short menu is a better use of the morning than a plated set that exists mainly to be photographed.

## How to use the spare hour

Every day in this plan has one gap: the walk between Asakusa and the river, the trees at Meiji Jingu, the East Gardens, or the last morning in Yanaka. Keep that gap. Tokyo is tiring when every hour is a reservation, and the neighborhoods above are close enough on the train that you do not need a fifth district to feel you have arrived.

Buy the Shibuya Sky ticket before the trip, carry a Suica from the first platform, and eat one sit-down meal a day from the table. The rest can be a station box or a market snack. That is enough for five days.
