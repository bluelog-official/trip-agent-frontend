# Prague: Where to Go in 2 Days (Weekend)
![A stunning aerial view of Prague highlighting the city's bridges and historical architecture.](https://images.pexels.com/photos/16309117/pexels-photo-16309117.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&dpr=2&h=650&w=940)
*Photo by [Jarod Barton](https://www.pexels.com/@jarod)*

Prague is one of Europe's most visually striking capitals, defined by gothic spires, cobblestone lanes, and centuries of preserved bohemian architecture. Because the city center is remarkably compact, a short weekend trip allows you to experience its iconic heritage sites alongside quieter, residential neighborhoods where authentic local culture thrives.

To make the most of forty-eight hours, avoid trying to check off every museum on the map. Instead, focus on a disciplined walking strategy that pairs major historical landmarks in the early morning with relaxed exploration of Prague’s distinct district dining scenes during the afternoon and evening hours.

## A Weekend in Prague

### Day 1 (Saturday)
Neighborhood Route: Start in the heart of Old Town (Staré Město), cross the Vltava River via Charles Bridge (Karlův most), and ascend through the winding alleys of Malá Strana (Lesser Town) to the Prague Castle (Pražský hrad) complex. After exploring the castle grounds and St. Vitus Cathedral, descend through the Wallenstein Garden and take tram 22 east to the leafy, residential district of Vinohrady for dinner and drinks away from the central tourist crowds.

Timing: Arrive at Old Town Square by 7:30 AM to beat the tour groups. Cross Charles Bridge by 8:30 AM. Spend 9:30 AM to 1:30 PM exploring Malá Strana and the Prague Castle complex. Dedicate 2:00 PM to 4:30 PM to Kampa Island and the riverside. Transition to Vinohrady around 6:00 PM for dinner.

Practical Warning: Beware of independent currency exchange booths around Old Town Square and Karlova Street offering "0% Commission." They often hide abysmal exchange rates in the fine print. Never exchange cash on the street, and if using an ATM, always select "Decline Conversion" to ensure your home bank calculates the transaction rate.

### Day 2 (Sunday)
Neighborhood Route: Begin in Josefov (the historic Jewish Quarter) to observe its preserved historic synagogues and quiet streets. Walk north across the Čech Bridge and climb the stairs to Letná Park (Letenské sady) for panoramic views over Prague's bridges. From Letná, drop down into Karlín, a former industrial district turned modern culinary hotspot. Conclude your weekend with a sunset walk at the Vyšehrad fortress grounds south of the city center.

Timing: Explore Josefov from 9:00 AM to 11:30 AM. Walk through Letná Park and visit the giant Metronome between 11:45 AM and 1:30 PM. Spend 2:00 PM to 4:30 PM sampling food and coffee across Karlín. Arrive at Vyšehrad by 5:30 PM to catch sunset over the Vltava River.

Practical Warning: Transit inspectors target popular tourist tram lines (specifically lines 9, 15, and 22) heavily on Sunday afternoons. If using paper tickets, you must validate them in the yellow punch boxes immediately upon stepping onto a tram or entering a metro station; inspectors do not accept plain tickets and will issue an unappealable 1,500 CZK fine on the spot.

## Getting Around

Prague possesses one of the most reliable and affordable public transit networks in Europe, managed by PID (Pražská integrovaná doprava). You do not need taxis or ride-share apps. The simplest way to pay is by tapping a contactless credit or debit card directly on the orange validation terminals located inside every tram and bus, as well as at metro station gates. A 24-hour pass costs 120 CZK, single 30-minute tickets cost 30 CZK, and 90-minute tickets cost 40 CZK. You can also download the official "PID Lítačka" app to purchase and activate digital tickets on your phone. Metro lines A (Green), B (Yellow), and C (Red) handle long-distance transfers quickly, while trams provide scenic overland travel across the Vltava.

## Local Tip

When ordering draft beer in a traditional Czech pub, pay attention to the style of pour rather than just picking a brand. Czechs view beer foam as an essential component of temperature control, carbonation, and freshness, not wasted glass space. If you want a standard pour with three fingers of wet, dense foam, ask for a "hladinka." If you prefer a smoother, less carbonated pour served in a full glass of thick foam intended to be enjoyed quickly before a meal, order a "šnyt." Avoid asking for beer without foam; bartenders consider a foam-free pour ("čochtan") flat and harsh, and locals rarely order it.
