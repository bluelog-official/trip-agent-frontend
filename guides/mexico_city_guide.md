# How to Spend 5 Days in Mexico City
![A stunning aerial view of Chapultepec Park with cityscape and mountains in the background.](https://images.pexels.com/photos/28577135/pexels-photo-28577135.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&dpr=2&h=650&w=940)
*Photo by [Erhart Fabian Castillo Castellanos](https://www.pexels.com/@erhart-fabian-castillo-castellanos-179012)*

Mexico City operates at a high altitude and an impressive, relentless momentum. For first-time visitors, the sheer scale of this valley—home to over nine million people in the city proper—can feel overwhelming if you try to cross it repeatedly in a single afternoon. The key to a successful trip lies in clustering your activities by neighborhood zones, respecting the afternoon traffic jams, and giving your body time to adjust to the elevation.

By focusing on distinct hubs like the historic core, leafy western colonies, and southern canal systems, you will experience the metropolis as a collection of accessible urban villages rather than an unmanageable sprawl. This guide offers a balanced five-day blueprint designed to minimize time spent stuck in gridlock while maximizing your exposure to the city's rich architecture, street food, and cultural landmarks.

## 5 Days in Mexico City

### Day 1: Centro Histórico and Muralism

Route: Begin at the vast Zócalo plaza, tour the Metropolitan Cathedral, and view the archaeological excavations at Templo Mayor. Walk west along the pedestrian Calle Madero to the Palacio de Bellas Artes and rest in Alameda Central park. Finish with a visit to the Murales de Rivera at the Secretaría de Educación Pública.
Timing: 8:30 AM to 4:00 PM. Start early to beat museum lines and avoid afternoon rains.
Practical Warning: Keep valuables concealed on crowded pedestrian avenues like Calle Madero, as distraction-based pickpocketing is common around street performances and store entrances.

### Day 2: Coyoacán and San Ángel

Route: Take a morning ride south to Coyoacán. Tour Frida Kahlo’s Casa Azul, stroll through the tree-lined Plaza Hidalgo, and sample tostadas at the Mercado de Coyoacán. In the afternoon, walk through the quiet cobblestone lanes of neighboring San Ángel to view the Diego Rivera and Frida Kahlo Studio Museum.
Timing: 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM. 
Practical Warning: You must reserve Casa Azul tickets online weeks in advance; walk-up tickets are not sold under any circumstances.

### Day 3: Bosque de Chapultepec and Polanco

Route: Dedicate your morning to the world-class Museo Nacional de Antropología. Walk through the wooded paths of Chapultepec Park up to Chapultepec Castle for elevated city views. In the late afternoon, exit north into Polanco to stroll down Avenida Presidente Masaryk and explore nearby art galleries.
Timing: 9:00 AM to 6:00 PM. Plan at least three hours for the Anthropology Museum alone.
Practical Warning: The Anthropology Museum and Chapultepec Castle are closed every Monday, so adjust your schedule accordingly.

### Day 4: Xochimilco Canals and University City

Route: Drive south to Embarcadero Cuemanco in Xochimilco for a traditional wooden *trajenera* boat ride along the ancient agricultural canals. On your return trip, stop at the UNAM main campus to admire Juan O'Gorman’s massive stone mosaic mural covering the Central Library facade.
Timing: 8:00 AM to 3:30 PM.
Practical Warning: Ignore unofficial bicycle guides outside Xochimilco docks who try to steer tourists to private, overpriced boat launches charging double the regulated rate.

### Day 5: Condesa, Roma Norte, and Sunset Views

Route: Spend a relaxed day wandering the Art Deco residential streets of Condesa and Roma Norte. Walk through Parque México, browse independent bookstores along Avenida Álvaro Obregón, and head into Colonia Juárez. End your trip with panoramic views from the observation deck of Torre Latinoamericana.
Timing: 10:00 AM to 8:00 PM.
Practical Warning: Earthquakes can trigger the city wide seismic alarm; if you hear a loud siren with the spoken phrase "alerta sísmica," immediately exit buildings to designated street safety spots.

## Getting Around

The simplest way to use public transit is with the Tarjeta de Movilidad Integrada. You can purchase and top up this reloadable card at any Metro or Metrobus station for 15 MXN (under $1 USD). It grants access to the Metro, Metrobus, Cablebús, and light rail systems. Metro rides cost just 5 MXN, allowing you to bypass street traffic entirely, but avoid the trains during peak commute hours (7:30–9:30 AM and 6:00–8:00 PM) when cars become intensely packed. For late-night travel or direct neighborhood-to-neighborhood trips, rideshare apps like Uber and DiDi are safe, reliable, and affordable.

## Local Tip

Most guidebooks emphasize tap water safety but fail to mention the combined physical impact of Mexico City’s high elevation (2,240 meters / 7,350 feet) and dry air. Dehydration happens much faster here, exacerbating altitude fatigue and headaches before you even realize you are thirsty. Buy multi-liter jugs of purified water from local OXXO stores for your room, keep an electrolyte drink like *Electrolit* in your daypack, and trim your alcohol intake for the first forty-eight hours—one cocktail at this altitude hits like two at sea level.
